\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) have become a widely used tool for building complex statistical models and reasoning about uncertainty~\cite{Moy2025Roulette,Holtzen2020Dice,DeRaedt2007ProbLog,Saad2021SPPL,Carpenter2017Stan,Salvatier2016PyMC3,Bingham2019Pyro,Dillon2017TFP,Tran2016Edward,Tolpin2016Anglican,Goodman2014WebPPL,Pfeffer2009Figaro,Minka2018InferNET,Ge2018Turing,CusumanoTowner2019Gen,Tehrani2020BeanMachine,Goodman2008Church}. The vast range of existing PPLs vary in terms of their expressiveness and inference power, as well as on the ease for verification. For instance, languages that support {\em continuous} distributions are useful in representing rich stochastic processes where expressiveness is essential, but pose challenges for the analysis, inference, and verification of probabilistic programs, as their semantics often involve reasoning over uncountable domains or integrals over high-dimensional spaces. In contrast, programs in PPLs that only allow {\em discrete} distributions are easier to reason about but their limited expressiveness makes it difficult to naturally model phenomena that inherently involve real-valued quantities. In this paper, we propose a type-directed framework to bridge the gap between expressiveness and ease of reasoning in continuous and discrete PPLs. 


We introduce \Slice{}, a framework featuring a probabilistic language with support for continuous distributions and a compiler that automatically translates continuous distributions into discrete ones. The compiled program is a fully discrete probabilistic program, allowing one to leverage the well-understood semantics, analysis techniques, and execution strategies developed for discrete languages, {\em while preserving the semantics of the original continuous model}.  We call this new approach \emph{exact discretization}. Specifically, \Slice{} uses a type system to track all the constant thresholds (or {\em cuts}) against which continuous variables are compared. These cuts are then used to discretize the continuous distributions into a finite number of intervals. For instance, our compiler transforms \uniform(0, 1) < 0.5 to \discrete(0.5, 0.5) < 1, where the continuous uniform distribution becomes a discrete choice with two equally likely outcomes. This type-directed discretization allows programmers to encode continuous models while retaining the tractability and formal clarity of discrete programs. 

These dependencies often span distant parts of the program: a value drawn from a continuous distribution might be compared against several constants across different branches, returned from a conditional, passed through a higher-order function, or stored in a data structure before the comparison occurs. \Slice{}'s type inference propagates each cut along those flows, ensuring that every continuous distribution retains the cuts induced by its future comparisons, which enables the models in \Cref{sec:examples}. 

The idea of discretization in probabilistic programming is not new in the literature. Most notably, Garg et al.~\cite{Garg2024BitBlast} introduced a novel approximate inference method for hybrid probabilistic programs that uses a binary {\em bit-blasting} representation of continuous random variables to convert them into discrete Boolean structures. Their approach, like other approximation schemes~\cite{Huang2021AQUA}, trades accuracy for tractability. %It is worth noting that bit-blasting has no loss of accuracy for so-called mixed-gamma distributions, but these do not include gaussian and beta distributions, which are prevalent in many applications. 
To avoid conflating these efforts, we adopt consistent terminology throughout the paper. We call \Slice{}'s semantics-preserving translation \emph{exact discretization}: the discrete program it produces induces exactly the same distribution as the source program. We refer to techniques such as bit-blasting or user-parameterized interval refinements as \emph{quantization}, because they approximate a continuous quantity with a finite representation whose precision is controlled by an external parameter rather than by program structure. Quantization methods can offer controllable error bounds, but their results depend on the chosen precision, whereas exact discretization has no approximation knob and preserves semantics by construction.
Another key difference is that the granularity of quantization is driven by an external precision parameter $b$, whereas ours is driven by program structure (e.g. the number of intervals arises from the set of constant thresholds the program uses) and automatically inferred.

The \Slice{} language is a statically-typed functional language that supports higher-order functions, recursion, pairs, lists, and mutable references. Our type-driven transformation handles these features, allowing programmers to write expressive, high-level models that can still be analyzed with discrete PPLs. Our approach has one important limitation: only programs where continuous variables are compared against constants can be fully discretized. Programs that compare continuous variables directly to each other cannot be fully discretized. 

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Backend Flexibility.} Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the overall architecture of the \Slice{} system. \Slice{}'s discretization is backend-agnostic: any discrete PPL that supports finite distributions can serve as a target. We demonstrate the practicality of our approach on the problem of exact inference for continuous programs. By integrating our approach with two backends \Dice{} and \Roulette{}, we show that \Dice{} provides good performance for first-order programs, achieving speedups of up to 90x compared to SPPL. However, Dice cannot handle higher-order functions or recursion. Roulette~\cite{Moy2025Roulette} supports these advanced features, enabling \Slice{} to perform exact inference on higher-order continuous programs. Together these backends cover first-order and higher-order models, and any discrete PPL that supports finite distributions can serve as an additional compilation target. 

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} After introducing \Slice{} by example (\Cref{sec:examples}), we present and evaluate our framework for type-directed discretization via the following contributions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.3cm,topsep=3pt]
    \item[Type System (\Cref{sec:language})] We introduce a type system that identifies which expressions are discretizable by analyzing how they are compared against constant values throughout the program.

    \item[Type Inference (\Cref{sec:type-inference})] We develop a type inference algorithm that automatically collects the set of constant thresholds needed for discretization for each expression.

\item[Exact Discretization (\Cref{sec:discretization})] We present a type-directed program transformation that soundly converts continuous distributions and their comparisons into discrete counterparts.

    \item[Soundness (\Cref{sec:soundness})] We prove that our discretization method is sound, establishing that discretization preserves the semantics of the original program. Our proof employs probabilistic  \katherine{check for better wording}, a technique for reasoning about program equivalences in probabilistic languages~\cite{Bizjak2015Step,Wand2018Contextual}.

    \item[Implementation and Evaluation (\Cref{sec:implem_eval})] We provide an open-source implementation of \Slice{} that compiles programs for the Dice and Roulette exact inference engines (\Cref{fig:architecture}). We conduct an empirical evaluation on a range of benchmarks, old and new, demonstrating that our approach is faster than prior symbolic techniques on hybrid programs while maintaining correctness.
\end{description}
